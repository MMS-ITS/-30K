Criticisms I withdraw

I raised these before obtaining the documents, working only from a chat summary. Reading the actual report shows it handles all three correctly. Recording them as withdrawn rather than quietly dropping them.

  1. "It applies 12 months of rent to a property placed in service in August"
    It does not. Page 4 states plainly that the full-year tables are a stabilized planning view and not a claim about 2026, and directs the reader to use actual 2026 rent and expenses. The labelling is correct.
  2. "It uses full-year depreciation on a mid-month asset"
    It presents both. Page 4 gives $8,727 full-year and $3,274 for August 2026, cites the 1.364% first-year percentage, and warns the percentage changes with the month. The near-coincidence with the $3,233 profit figure is exactly that — a coincidence; both numbers are independently correct.
  3. "It confuses the standard deduction with rental interest"
    The opposite. Page 3 explains that Schedule E rental expenses are above-the-line and that he "does not need to itemize personal deductions merely to deduct legitimate rental expenses." That is right, and it is the distinction I accused it of missing.

What survives — and what only the documents reveal

  1. The $2,000 draft contradicts the $2,800 property tax, and nobody resolved it
    The draft implies $7,571/year of escrow. Against a stated $2,800 property tax that leaves $4,771 for insurance — implausible for a $300,000 rental. Read the other way, a normal $1,800 premium implies property tax near $5,771, or 1.92% of price, squarely in the Texas range. A newly purchased rental also loses any homestead cap and exemption the prior owner held, so a reassessment to roughly that level is exactly what you would expect. The report identified the right question and stopped one step short of the answer.
  2. Resolving it reverses the report's headline conclusion
    The executive verdict states the property produces "a small taxable profit, not a tax loss." With the escrow-implied tax and an ordinary insurance premium, the same model yields a $1,539 loss — and the "$711 of additional federal tax" becomes roughly $339 of tax reduction. That is a swing of about $1,050 on the report's own structure, using only its own disclosed evidence.
  3. The recast column understates by $91,331 because it assumes you never overpay
    Page 7 shows recast with $242,448 of lifetime interest against $151,117 for keeping the payment — making recast look like it saves $40,408 versus $131,738. But recasting does not oblige you to pay the lower amount. Recast, then voluntarily keep paying $1,369.04, and lifetime interest is $151,117 — identical — while you retain the right to drop to $1,173.47. The table's structure biases the verdict against the option that is actually better, and the verdict duly recommends against it.
  4. A pre-tax retirement contribution is never compared
    Page 5's own footnote says the tax illustration "excludes 401(k), HSA" — acknowledging the vehicle exists, then never testing it. At a 22% marginal rate, $30,000 of unused pre-tax room is worth about $6,600 immediately against a 5.30% hurdle. It is not close, and it belongs in the option set.
  5. "Substantial reserves" is asserted, and it is doing real work
    Page 8 concludes "liquidity is less of a constraint in the fact pattern provided" — but no reserve figure appears anywhere in ten pages. Page 9 then lists weakened reserves as something that would change the verdict. So an unmeasured input is load-bearing. Combined with H-1B status, where authorization is employment-contingent with a short grace period, and a first rental year carrying vacancy and turnover risk at once, this deserved a number rather than an adjective.
  6. Active participation is treated as near-certain when the facts strain it
    Page 6 correctly notes a property manager does not automatically defeat active participation. True — but the owner is in Nevada, the property is in Texas, and management is outsourced at $3,800/year. That is the fact pattern where substantiation is hardest. It matters more than the report suggests: if active participation fails, the loss suspends entirely, the interest deduction has no current value, and the after-tax cost of the mortgage rises from 5.30% to the full 6.80% — which makes prepayment more attractive, not less. The conclusion survives; the stated reason for it does not.
  7. The nominal figure leads, and the discounted one never appears
    Partial credit: page 8 does carry the 5.30% opportunity-cost check, which is the right frame. But pages 1 and 9 both headline $131,738. Discounted at the loan's own rate the present value of prepaying is exactly zero — the present value of a loan's remaining payments is its balance, so retiring $30,000 of balance buys $30,000 of value. Leading with an undiscounted 20-year sum makes a sound-but-ordinary 5.30% return look like a windfall, and crowds out the liquidity and retirement questions that should outrank it.
  8. Two different savings figures were published
    The chat gave $131,072, then $131,738. The PDF is right at $131,738.50; the earlier number was never retracted. Small in dollars, but an unexplained change to the headline figure.
  9. Depreciation recapture is described but never priced
    Pages 4 and 10 explain unrecaptured §1250 gain and the 25% ceiling correctly. Neither the report nor the app ever subtracts it, so depreciation still reads as a pure benefit rather than a deferral with a quantifiable cost at exit.
  10. The interactive app cannot test the sensitivity both documents rely on
    It exposes eleven inputs — price, down payment, rate, term, extra principal, rent, property tax, management, HOA, building share, tax rate — and no field for insurance, repairs or vacancy. Both documents state that unlisted expenses above $3,233 flip profit to loss, and that landlord insurance alone may do it. The tool provided to test that claim structurally cannot.
  11. Smaller items
    Page 6 says he is "near the full-allowance range" at $95,000 — he is below $100,000 and squarely inside it, so the hedge is unnecessary. §199A is never mentioned, though a rental rising to a trade or business may support a deduction against any profit. The app applies the curtailment at month 1 while the PDF applies it at month 0, a $0.17/month divergence in the recast payment — immaterial, but the two artefacts do not agree.
